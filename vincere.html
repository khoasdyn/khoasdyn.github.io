Vincere: Five Years Designing a Recruitment OS

Vincere is a B2B recruitment platform used by agencies in Australia, the UK, Europe and Japan. I spent five years there as a product designer, in a team of four that owned every screen and the design system behind them. This is what that work looked like, including the year it changed completely.

Project Overview

I joined Vincere in August 2021 and left in June 2026. The company was already about fifteen years old when I arrived. Around 2022 the CEO sold it to The Access Group, and from that point the product was one part of a much larger software group.

The design team was three people for my first two years, two product designers and a lead, and four after that. Between us we covered all of the UI and UX, the design system, and the occasional landing page. There was no separate research team, no UX writer and no design ops.

The product people use every day is a web app. There are mobile apps too, but they were never the focus.

The Product

Vincere is positioned as a Recruitment OS. It is not one app but an ecosystem, with more than ten smaller products inside it serving recruiters, admins, contacts and candidates. Its surface is wide enough that knowing all of it was never realistic for anyone, including the people who had been there longest.

The interfaces are dry by nature. Tables, charts, statistics, and flows with a lot of branches. Every view, every screen, every dropdown carries a large amount of information, options and settings.

That was deliberate. The direction from Vincere, and later from The Access Group, was to give customers as much choice and as much customisation as possible, so that one platform could fit very different ways of running a recruitment agency. As a designer that constraint runs in the opposite direction to most advice you read. The job was rarely to remove options. It was to make a screen full of them stay usable.

I was there five years. Some product managers had been there ten. Nobody knew the whole product.

Working Without Users

The customers are recruitment agencies and headhunting firms, almost all of them outside Vietnam. In five years I never spoke to one directly. That work belonged to the product managers and the Head of Product, who sat between the design team, engineering, the customers and the stakeholders.

So requirements arrived as tickets, and feedback came back the same way. This is the honest shape of the role and I am not going to dress it up as user research I did not do.

What it changed is where the questions had to go. If a ticket did not explain who the screen was for or what problem it solved, the only way to find out was to book time with the product manager and ask before drawing anything. Doing that early was the difference between a design that answered the real request and one that had to be redone after review.

How the Work Moved

From 2021 until the middle of 2025 the team ran two-week Agile sprints, and a ticket travelled the same road every time. A product manager wrote it. The lead designer assigned it. I clarified anything unclear with the product manager, then worked out the user flow and wireframes in FigJam before opening Figma.

Then the design itself: mockups, prototypes, components. A review with the lead designer, then a review with the product manager, then handoff to the developers. It was a predictable process, and predictable is worth a lot on a product where no single person knows every corner.

The Design System

A product this wide falls apart without a design system, and four designers cannot hand-draw ten products into consistency. So the system was the work that made the rest of the work possible.

The four of us built and maintained it in Figma: close to a thousand components, screens, libraries and views, covering more than ten products inside the platform. It is the piece of my five years I am most confident about, because its value is measurable in every screen that did not need a decision made twice.

It is also where my Figma craft actually came from. Variables, styles, variants and properties, strict Auto-Layout, file organisation that someone else can navigate, and plugins to remove the repetitive parts. I can move from an idea to a working prototype quickly now, and that speed is a by-product of the system rather than a talent.

Sitting next to developers and testers for five years taught me the other half. I picked up enough HTML, CSS and React to understand how the product was actually built, how the code was organised, and which design decisions were expensive to implement. I learned where performance and data fetching quietly set the limits on an interface.

That changed my handoffs more than anything else. I know what a developer needs before they can start, so I give them the states, the edge cases and the flow in one place, rather than a screen and a conversation.

When the Process Broke

In June 2025 The Access Group started merging its companies into a single ecosystem called Access Evo. Overnight the design team was no longer working only on Vincere. Work arrived from other companies in the group, and Vincere itself went quiet.

That stretch was genuinely disordered. There was no agreed process, no clear queue of features, and no shared understanding of how many steps it took to close a ticket. Work came from whoever handed it to you.

I am including this because it is the truthful middle of the story. The neat sprint process I described above is the part that is easy to put in a portfolio. Most of my last year was the other thing, and being useful inside it was a different skill: keeping my own structure when the company had stopped providing one.

No process, no queue, no agreed definition of done. Whoever handed you work, that was the work.

Designing in the Codebase

From January 2026 until I left in June, the team stopped designing mockups in Figma. Not fewer mockups. None. The design step moved into the codebase, written as prompts and direct edits with AI.

The new loop was short. I worked with the product manager and Claude to build a micro-frontend inside the team's own repository on Azure DevOps. Once there were working versions, we reviewed them with the lead designer and the product manager. When the micro-frontend was agreed, a developer pushed it to production.

The work varied. Some of it was UI and UX improvement with no change to the flow or the business rules. Some of it was a new feature or a new product. Some was carrying an old Vincere feature across into the new one, and some was refining a prototype a product manager or a stakeholder had already built.

What I liked about it is that a review argument ends faster when the thing under discussion is real. Nobody has to imagine how a prototype would behave with real data, because it is already behaving.

None of that would have been possible a year earlier. The company rolled AI out to every role in August 2025 and ran training on using it properly, which is when I got my own Claude account. Within about ten months it had moved through most of my work: research, generating design directions, writing documents, building prototypes, and checking whether a flow holds up.

The less obvious gain was the vocabulary. Working in a real repository meant learning branches, pull requests, merges, pushes and pulls, so I now understand how a digital product is built and released rather than only how it is drawn.

What I Took From It

Five years on one product teaches you patience with complexity. I stopped expecting to simplify my way out of a hard screen, because on this product the complexity was the point. The customers were paying for it.

The design system taught me that consistency is infrastructure. Four people covered ten products only because the decisions were made once and held in a place everyone could reach.

And the last year taught me not to be attached to the tool. I spent five years getting good at Figma and then watched the design step move somewhere else entirely. Being able to follow it into the codebase, instead of defending the file I was comfortable in, is the part of this job I would want to do again.
